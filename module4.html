Interpolation & Polynomial Approximation

Apply interpolation methods and polynomial approximation. Here the curve must pass through every data point. We build interpolating polynomials (Lagrange, Newton), see why high degree is dangerous, and switch to piecewise splines.

Interpolation vs. extrapolation

🔊 Listen to this section

Interpolation estimates values between known data points; extrapolation estimates outside the data range. Unlike least-squares fitting (Module 3), interpolation assumes the data is exact and requires the curve to pass through every point.

Interpolation (Module 4)
Data assumed exact · curve hits every point · use when points come from a precise table or formula.
Regression (Module 3)
Data assumed noisy · curve captures the trend · use when points are measurements with error.
⚠️ Extrapolate with care
Predicting outside the data range is far riskier than interpolating inside it — interpolating polynomials can swing wildly just beyond the last point.

4.1 Polynomial interpolation

🔊 Listen to this section
📐 Key words, in plain language
Data points are measured or tabulated pairs \((x_i, y_i)\). Interpolation means finding a function that passes exactly through all of them, so we can estimate \(y\) at an \(x\) that was not measured. That function is called the interpolant (here, an interpolating polynomial). A polynomial is a sum of powers of \(x\), such as \(2+3x-x^2\); its degree is the highest power.

Through any \(n\) data points with distinct \(x\)-values, there is exactly one polynomial of degree at most \(n-1\) that passes through all of them. Two points define a unique line; three points a unique parabola; and so on.

\[ P(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots + a_{n-1} x^{n-1}. \]
Each extra point raises the degree by one: 2 points fix a line, 3 a parabola, 4 a cubic. In every case exactly one polynomial passes through all the points.

The unknowns here are the \(n\) coefficients \(a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}\). Each data point gives one equation, \(P(x_i)=y_i\), so we get \(n\) equations for \(n\) unknowns, a linear system we can solve.

You could find the coefficients by solving a linear system (a Vandermonde system), but that matrix is often ill-conditioned. The Lagrange and Newton forms construct the same unique polynomial more reliably.

💡 What does "ill-conditioned" mean?
A problem is ill-conditioned when tiny changes in the input (a rounding error, a slightly noisy \(y\)-value) cause huge changes in the answer. The Vandermonde matrix is a classic example: its columns \(1, x, x^2, \dots, x^{n-1}\) look almost identical to each other as the degree grows, so the system is nearly singular. Computers store numbers with limited precision, so the computed coefficients \(a_i\) can be badly wrong — even though the equations were "solved" correctly — and the resulting curve may miss the data points. Lagrange and Newton avoid this because they never build or solve that matrix: Lagrange writes the answer directly as a sum of basis polynomials, and Newton builds it step by step from divided differences. They need only simple additions, multiplications and divisions, which keeps rounding errors small and is also cheaper than solving a full linear system.
Why the Vandermonde matrix is ill-conditioned: its columns are \(1,x,x^2,\dots\). For high powers these curves are nearly indistinguishable, so the computer cannot tell the columns apart reliably.
🧮 An everyday analogy
Imagine trying to find where two almost-parallel lines cross. Nudge one line by a hair and the crossing point jumps far away. Nearly identical columns make the Vandermonde system behave the same way.

4.2 Lagrange interpolating polynomials

🔊 Listen to this section

Big idea: instead of solving for coefficients, build the answer from simple "switch" pieces. Each piece is 1 at one data point and 0 at all the others, so multiplying it by that point's \(y\)-value "switches on" exactly one data point.

The Lagrange form writes the interpolant (the interpolating polynomial \(P(x)\) that passes through every data point) as a weighted sum of the data values:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(x) &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i\, L_i(x),\\[4pt] L_i(x) &= \prod_{\substack{j=1 \\ j\neq i}}^{n} \frac{x - x_j}{x_i - x_j}. \end{aligned} \]

Each basis polynomial \(L_i(x)\) is cleverly built to equal \(1\) at its own node \(x_i\) and \(0\) at every other node. So at \(x=x_k\) only the \(k\)-th term survives and \(P(x_k)=y_k\) — the curve hits every point by construction.

🔎 How to read \(L_i(x)\)
The numerator \(\prod (x-x_j)\) contains a factor that is zero at every other node, so \(L_i\) vanishes there. The denominator \(\prod (x_i-x_j)\) is just a number that rescales the piece so that \(L_i(x_i)=1\). The symbol \(\prod\) means "multiply all of these", just as \(\sum\) means "add all of these".

See it with numbers: why \(L_1\) is 0 at \(x=2\) and \(x=3\), and 1 at \(x=1\)

🔊 Listen to this example

Data used in this section: the three points \((1,1),\ (2,4),\ (3,9)\), so the nodes are \(x_1=1,\ x_2=2,\ x_3=3\) and the values are \(y_1=1,\ y_2=4,\ y_3=9\). Notice that the basis polynomials \(L_i\) use only the \(x\)-values; the \(y\)-values enter later, as the weights in the sum.

The first basis polynomial is a product of two factors, one for each other node (\(x_2=2\) and \(x_3=3\)):

\[ L_1(x)=\frac{(x-2)\,(x-3)}{(1-2)\,(1-3)}=\frac{(x-2)(x-3)}{2}. \]

Each factor \((x-x_j)\) becomes zero exactly when \(x\) equals that node \(x_j\). One zero factor makes the whole product zero. Substitute the three nodes, one after the other, as you would on paper:

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1(1)&=\frac{(1-2)(1-3)}{2}=\frac{(-1)(-2)}{2}=\frac{2}{2}=\mathbf{1},\\[6pt] L_1(2)&=\frac{(2-2)(2-3)}{2}=\frac{\,0\cdot(-1)\,}{2}=\mathbf{0},\\[6pt] L_1(3)&=\frac{(3-2)(3-3)}{2}=\frac{1\cdot 0}{2}=\mathbf{0}. \end{aligned} \]

At its own node \(x=1\) the top equals the bottom (both are \(2\)), so the fraction is exactly \(1\): that is why the denominator was chosen as \((1-2)(1-3)\). At the other two nodes one factor on top is \(0\), so the fraction is \(0\).

This is exactly what the left graph shows for the teal curve \(L_1\): it passes through height 1 at \(x=1\) and through height 0 at \(x=2\) and \(x=3\). The same recipe works for \(L_2\) (factors \((x-1)(x-3)\)) and \(L_3\) (factors \((x-1)(x-2)\)): leave out the factor of your own node, keep one factor for every other node.

Left: the three basis polynomials for the nodes 1, 2, 3. Right: multiply each by its \(y\)-value (1, 4, 9) and add. The sum is \(x^2\) and passes through all three red points.

Worked example (written as on an exam)

🔊 Listen to this example

Given: the data points \((1,1),\ (2,4),\ (3,9)\).
Find: the interpolating polynomial \(P(x)\) in Lagrange form, and estimate \(P(2.5)\).

Solution.

Step 1. There are \(n=3\) points, so \(P(x)=y_1L_1(x)+y_2L_2(x)+y_3L_3(x)\).

Step 2. Basis polynomials (leave out your own node's factor, divide by its value at your own node):

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1(x)&=\frac{(x-2)(x-3)}{(1-2)(1-3)}=\frac{(x-2)(x-3)}{2}=\frac{x^2-5x+6}{2},\\[4pt] L_2(x)&=\frac{(x-1)(x-3)}{(2-1)(2-3)}=\frac{(x-1)(x-3)}{-1}=-(x^2-4x+3),\\[4pt] L_3(x)&=\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}{(3-1)(3-2)}=\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}{2}=\frac{x^2-3x+2}{2}. \end{aligned} \]

Step 3. Weight each by its \(y\)-value and add:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(x)&=1\cdot\frac{x^2-5x+6}{2}\;+\;4\cdot\bigl(-(x^2-4x+3)\bigr)\;+\;9\cdot\frac{x^2-3x+2}{2}\\[4pt] &=\frac{(x^2-5x+6)-8(x^2-4x+3)+9(x^2-3x+2)}{2}\\[4pt] &=\frac{(1-8+9)\,x^2+(-5+32-27)\,x+(6-24+18)}{2}=\frac{2x^2+0\cdot x+0}{2}=x^2. \end{aligned} \]

Step 4. Check: \(P(1)=1,\ P(2)=4,\ P(3)=9\) ✓. Estimate: \(P(2.5)=2.5^2=6.25\).

Final answer: the interpolating polynomial (an equation) is

\[ \boxed{P(x)=x^2}, \]

and the requested estimate (a number) is

\[ \boxed{P(2.5)=6.25}. \]

4.3 Newton's divided-difference form

🔊 Listen to this section

Newton's form builds the same polynomial incrementally, which makes it cheap to add a new data point without recomputing everything:

\[ P(x) = b_0 + b_1(x-x_0) + b_2(x-x_0)(x-x_1) + \cdots \]

Big idea: start with a constant that matches the first point, then add a line to also match the second, then a parabola to also match the third, and so on. Each new term is multiplied by factors that are zero at the earlier nodes, so it does not disturb the points already matched.

Important: there is only one curve at the end. Each step takes the current curve (grey) and adds a correction term (orange dashed) to it, giving the new curve (teal). The correction is exactly 0 at every node already matched, so those points stay on the curve, while at the new node it supplies just the missing height.

Newton's form for the points (0,1), (1,3), (2,2). Grey: the curve from the previous step. Orange dashed: the term added in this step; it is zero at the earlier points (look where it crosses \(y=0\)). Teal: the new curve = grey + orange. Red dots are the points matched so far.

The coefficients \(b_k\) are divided differences, computed from a triangular table. A first divided difference is just a slope; a second divided difference is the change in slope, divided by the width. Higher ones are slopes of slopes.

\[ \begin{aligned} f[x_i,x_j] &= \frac{f(x_j)-f(x_i)}{x_j-x_i},\\[4pt] f[x_i,x_j,x_k] &= \frac{f[x_j,x_k]-f[x_i,x_j]}{x_k-x_i},\ \dots \end{aligned} \]

Worked example

Problem. Find the interpolating polynomial through \((0,1),\ (1,3),\ (2,2)\) using Newton's form, then estimate \(P(0.5)\).

Step 1: build the divided-difference table. Write the \(y\)-values in the first column. Each next column is computed from the column to its left: (difference of neighbours) divided by (the \(x\)-distance they span).

Step 2: read off the coefficients. They are the bold numbers along the top edge: \(b_0=1,\ b_1=2,\ b_2=-1.5\). So

\[ P(x)=1+2(x-0)-1.5\,(x-0)(x-1). \]

Step 3: check it. At \(x=0\) only \(b_0\) survives: \(P(0)=1\) ✓. At \(x=1\): \(1+2(1)-1.5(1)(0)=3\) ✓. At \(x=2\): \(1+4-1.5(2)(1)=2\) ✓. The curve hits all three points.

Step 4: estimate \(P(0.5)\).

\[ P(0.5)=1+2(0.5)-1.5(0.5)(0.5-1)=1+1+0.375=\mathbf{2.375}. \]

Step 5: add a new point cheaply. Suppose a fourth point \((3,5)\) arrives. Keep everything above and add one term \(b_3(x-0)(x-1)(x-2)\). Currently \(P(3)=1+6-1.5(3)(2)=-2\), but we need 5, so the gap is \(7\). The new factor equals \(3\cdot2\cdot1=6\) at \(x=3\), hence \(b_3=7/6\approx1.167\). Nothing already computed had to change, which is exactly why Newton's form is convenient when data arrive one at a time.

Lagrange and Newton give identical polynomials — they are just two ways to write the unique interpolant. Newton is preferred when points arrive one at a time; Lagrange is conceptually cleaner.

4.4 The problem: high-degree interpolation oscillates

🔊 Listen to this section

It is tempting to fit many points with one high-degree polynomial. But high-degree interpolants oscillate wildly between equally spaced points — especially near the ends of the interval. This is Runge's phenomenon, classically shown with

\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{1+25x^2} \quad\text{on}\quad [-1,1]. \]

As you add equally spaced nodes, the interpolating polynomial fits the nodes exactly yet swings further and further from \(f\) between them. See it live: in the demo after Section 4.5, press Load Runge function, choose Lagrange polynomial and raise the node count.

⚠️ Why does this happen?
A polynomial of degree \(n-1\) can have up to \(n-2\) bends. To thread through many points it must bend a lot, and the bends are largest near the ends. "Passing through the points" does not mean "staying close to the true curve in between".
➡️ So what is the solution?
The problem is the single high-degree polynomial. The fix is not to raise the degree but to lower it and use many low-degree pieces, one per interval. That is exactly what splines do, and it is the topic of the next section.

4.5 The solution: piecewise & spline interpolation

🔊 Listen to this section

Instead of one global polynomial, a spline joins many low-degree polynomials, one per interval between data points. Because each piece only has to fit two neighbouring points, no piece needs to bend wildly.

  • Linear spline: connect adjacent points with straight segments. Simple, but kinked (the slope jumps at each node).
  • Cubic spline: a separate cubic on each interval, chosen so the pieces meet smoothly — matching value, first derivative, and second derivative at every interior node. The result is smooth and avoids Runge oscillation.
🔎 What "smooth" means here
"Matching the first derivative" means the slopes of two neighbouring pieces agree at the joint, so there is no corner. "Matching the second derivative" means the curvature agrees too, so the bending does not suddenly change. Think of a flexible ruler pinned at the data points: it bends smoothly, and that shape is a cubic spline.
📐 Why cubic?
Cubic is the lowest degree that can match position and curvature smoothly at the joints. That is why cubic splines are the standard for smooth interpolation of tabulated data.

✏️ 4.4 The danger of high-degree interpolation
You have 20 noisy measurements. Fit one degree-19 interpolating polynomial, or least-squares fit a low-degree curve?
Reveal answer
Least-squares fit. A high-degree polynomial forced through every point oscillates wildly between them — Runge's phenomenon — especially near the ends. Regression follows the trend without chasing the noise. high degree ≠ better; interpolate only exact data, and keep the degree low
✏️ 4.5 Piecewise & spline interpolation
For 11 exact points, why prefer a cubic spline over one degree-10 polynomial? What smoothness does a cubic spline guarantee?
Reveal answer
A spline uses a separate low-degree (cubic) piece between each pair of points, so it cannot develop the large end-oscillations of a single high-degree polynomial.
At every interior knot it matches value, first derivative, and second derivative (\(C^{2}\) continuity), so the curve looks smooth. A "natural" spline sets the second derivative to zero at the two ends. many smooth low-degree pieces beat one high-degree curve
✏️ 4.6 MATLAB's built-in interpolation
Which built-in interpolates a 1-D data set at query points xq? How do you ask for linear vs spline?
Reveal answer
yq = interp1(x, y, xq, 'linear');   % straight-line pieces
yq = interp1(x, y, xq, 'spline');   % smooth cubic spline
yq = interp1(x, y, xq, 'pchip');    % shape-preserving cubic
The method string picks the scheme; 'spline' is the \(C^{2}\) cubic spline from 4.5. interp1 for 1-D; interp2 for surfaces
