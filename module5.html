Numerical Integration & ODEs

Solve differential equations using numerical integration. We approximate definite integrals by summing simple shapes, then use the same idea to march the solution of an ordinary differential equation forward in time.

📘 Chapra Ch. 19, 20, 22, 23 ⏱ Weeks 12–15 🧪 Assessed by Quiz 2, Lab final & Final

The definite integral as area

🔊 Listen to this section

A definite integral is the area under the curve between two limits:

\[ I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

For complicated or non-elementary functions there is often no analytic antiderivative. Numerical integration (quadrature) approximates the area by breaking \([a,b]\) into subintervals and summing the areas of simple shapes — rectangles, trapezoids, or parabola-capped strips — that hug the curve.

All the rules below split the interval into \(n\) segments of width

\[ \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}. \]

5.1 The midpoint rule

🔊 Listen to this section

Approximate each strip by a rectangle whose height is the function value at the midpoint of that strip:

\[ I \approx \Delta x \sum_{k} f(m_k), \qquad m_k = \text{midpoint of strip } k. \]
  1. Compute \(\Delta x = (b-a)/n\).
  2. Find the midpoint of each subinterval.
  3. Evaluate \(f\) at every midpoint, sum, and multiply by \(\Delta x\).

Worked example

🔊 Listen to this example

Approximate \(\int_0^{10} x^3\,dx\) with \(n=5\). Then \(\Delta x = 2\) and the midpoints are \(1,3,5,7,9\):

\[ S = 1^3+3^3+5^3+7^3+9^3 = 1+27+125+343+729 = 1225, \]
\[ I \approx \Delta x \cdot S = 2(1225) = 2450 \quad(\text{exact } 2500,\ \text{error } 2\%). \]

📈 See it: the integration demo after 5.3 (midpoint, \(x^3\), \(n=5\)).

5.2 The trapezoidal rule

🔊 Listen to this section

Connect adjacent points with straight lines and sum the resulting trapezoids. The interior points are counted twice (each is shared by two trapezoids), giving the weighting \(1,2,2,\dots,2,1\):

\[ I \approx \frac{\Delta x}{2}\Bigl[\,f(x_0) + 2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} f(x_i) + f(x_n)\,\Bigr]. \]

Worked example

🔊 Listen to this example

Approximate \(\int_0^{8} x^2\,dx\) with \(n=4\), so \(\Delta x = 2\) and grid points \(0,2,4,6,8\):

\[ S = f(0)+f(8) + 2\bigl[f(2)+f(4)+f(6)\bigr] = 0 + 64 + 2(4+16+36) = 176, \]
\[ I \approx \frac{\Delta x}{2}\,S = \frac{2}{2}(176) = 176 \quad(\text{exact } 170.67). \]

📈 See it: the integration demo after 5.3 (trapezoidal, \(x^2\), \(n=4\)).

5.3 Simpson's 1/3 rule

🔊 Listen to this section

Instead of straight lines, fit a parabola through each pair of strips. This is far more accurate for smooth functions. With an even number of segments, the weighting pattern is \(1,4,2,4,\dots,4,1\):

\[ I \approx \frac{\Delta x}{3}\Bigl[\,f(x_0) + 4\!\!\sum_{i\,\text{odd}}\!\! f(x_i) + 2\!\!\sum_{i\,\text{even}}\!\! f(x_i) + f(x_n)\,\Bigr]. \]
💡 Accuracy ladder
Midpoint & trapezoid are exact for straight lines (low order). Simpson is exact for cubics — for the same number of points it is typically far more accurate. That extra accuracy comes free, just from smarter weights.

📈 See the parabolas in the integration demo after this section (Simpson, \(x^2\), \(n=4\)).

Counting the weights: where do the 1, 4, 2 come from?

With \(n\) segments you have \(n+1\) points \(x_0,\dots,x_n\). Each parabola uses three points (two segments), so the segments must pair up: \(n\) must be even, i.e. you need an odd number of points (3, 5, 7, 9, …).

\[ \begin{array}{c|c|l} \text{points} & n & \text{weights } (\times\,\Delta x/3)\\ \hline 3 & 2 & 1,\,4,\,1\\ 5 & 4 & 1,\,4,\,2,\,4,\,1\\ 7 & 6 & 1,\,4,\,2,\,4,\,2,\,4,\,1\\ 9 & 8 & 1,\,4,\,2,\,4,\,2,\,4,\,2,\,4,\,1 \end{array} \]

Every parabola contributes the same little pattern \(1,4,1\) (left end, middle, right end). Adding the patterns of neighbouring parabolas: the first and last points belong to only one parabola, so they keep weight \(1\); each middle point of a parabola (odd index) belongs to one parabola only, weight \(4\); each point where two parabolas meet (even interior index, like \(x_2\) when \(n=4\)) is counted by both, so \(1+1=\mathbf{2}\).

⚠️ Four points (n = 3)? It is not 1, 4, 4, 1
Four points means \(n=3\) segments, an odd number, so the segments cannot be paired into parabolas and Simpson's 1/3 rule does not apply. The tempting list \(1,4,4,1\) is simply wrong. Your options:
  • Simpson's 3/8 rule (one cubic through all four points): \(\displaystyle I\approx\frac{3\Delta x}{8}\bigl[f_0+3f_1+3f_2+f_3\bigr]\), weights \(1,3,3,1\). It is also exact for cubics.
  • Mix: Simpson's 1/3 on the first two segments, then one trapezoid on the last.
  • Change \(n\): add or drop a point so that \(n\) is even.
🛠 Quick checks & hints
  • The weights must add up to \(3n\). Integrating \(f=1\) must give \(b-a=n\,\Delta x\), and \(\tfrac{\Delta x}{3}\sum w_i = n\Delta x\) forces \(\sum w_i=3n\). Check: \(1+4+2+4+1=12=3\cdot4\) ✓. But \(1+4+4+1=10\ne 9\) ✗ — that is how you can see that \(1,4,4,1\) is wrong. (For the 3/8 rule: \(1+3+3+1=8\) and the prefactor is \(3\Delta x/8\), so it works too.)
  • Odd index = 4, even interior index = 2 (counting from \(x_0\)). In MATLAB, with y(1) = \(f_0\), the 4's are y(2:2:end-1) and the 2's are y(3:2:end-2).

5.4 Gaussian & adaptive quadrature

🧭 Where we are
In 5.1 to 5.3 the sample points were equally spaced. We only chose how many points. This section makes two smarter choices: Gaussian quadrature chooses where to sample, and adaptive quadrature chooses where to use more strips.

5.4a Gaussian quadrature: choose the sample points cleverly

🔊 Listen to this section

Every rule so far has the same shape: sample the function at a few points, multiply each sample by a weight, add up. With equally spaced points we are free to choose only the weights. Gaussian quadrature also lets us choose where the points go. With \(n\) free positions and \(n\) free weights we have \(2n\) numbers to tune, and that is enough to integrate every polynomial up to degree \(2n-1\) exactly.

📐 The formula
On the standard interval \([-1,1]\):
\[ \int_{-1}^{1} f(t)\,dt \;\approx\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\, f(t_i). \]
The numbers \(t_i\) (the nodes) and \(w_i\) (the weights) are fixed constants. You look them up once, in the table below, and reuse them for every integral.
\(n\)nodes \(t_i\)weights \(w_i\)exact for degree up to
1021

Same two function values, very different answer. The trapezoid rule samples at the ends and overshoots (3.0862). Gauss samples at \(\pm0.577\): the line through those two points lies above the curve in the middle and below it near the ends, and the gain and the loss almost cancel (2.3427, exact 2.3504).

Where do these numbers come from?

Take \(n=2\): unknowns \(w_1,w_2,t_1,t_2\). Demand that the rule is exact for \(1,\ t,\ t^2,\ t^3\), four equations for four unknowns:

\[ w_1+w_2=2,\quad w_1t_1+w_2t_2=0,\quad w_1t_1^2+w_2t_2^2=\tfrac23,\quad w_1t_1^3+w_2t_2^3=0. \]

The right-hand sides are \(\int_{-1}^{1}t^k\,dt\). By symmetry take \(t_2=-t_1\) and \(w_1=w_2\); the first equation gives \(w_1=w_2=1\), and the third becomes \(2t_1^2=\tfrac23\), so \(t_1=1/\sqrt3\approx0.5774\). For larger \(n\) the same idea works, and the nodes turn out to be the roots of the Legendre polynomial \(P_n\). (For example \(P_2(t)=\tfrac12(3t^2-1)\) has roots \(\pm1/\sqrt3\).) In practice you never solve this yourself: software stores the table.

Other intervals \([a,b]\)

Stretch and shift the standard interval with \(x=\tfrac{b-a}{2}\,t+\tfrac{a+b}{2}\). Then \(dx=\tfrac{b-a}{2}dt\), so

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;\approx\; \frac{b-a}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\, f\!\Big(\tfrac{b-a}{2}\,t_i+\tfrac{a+b}{2}\Big). \]

Worked example

🔊 Listen to this example

Problem. Use 2-point Gauss to estimate \(\int_0^4 (x^2+1)\,dx\) (the integral from the practice section; exact value \(25.333\)).

Step 1: map the nodes to \([0,4]\). Here \(\tfrac{b-a}{2}=2\) and \(\tfrac{a+b}{2}=2\), so \(x_i=2t_i+2\): \(x_1=2-1.1547=0.8453\) and \(x_2=2+1.1547=3.1547\).

Step 2: evaluate. \(f(0.8453)=0.8453^2+1=1.7145\) and \(f(3.1547)=3.1547^2+1=10.9521\).

Step 3: weight and add. \(\tfrac{b-a}{2}(w_1f_1+w_2f_2)=2\,(1\cdot1.7145+1\cdot10.9521)=\mathbf{25.3333}\). This is exact, as promised, since \(x^2+1\) has degree \(2\le 3\). Only two function evaluations were needed.

5.4b Adaptive quadrature: spend effort where the function is hard

🔊 Listen to this section

What is the "wiggly part"?

A Simpson parabola (5.3) follows a curve well when the curve is smooth and gently bending. Where the curve bends sharply or oscillates quickly (a spike, a steep start like \(\sqrt{x}\) near \(0\), or fast wiggles), one parabola per strip cannot follow it, and the strip's error is large. Those are the wiggly (hard) parts. Mathematically the Simpson error on a strip of width \(h\) is about

\[ E \approx -\frac{h^5}{2880}\,f^{(4)}(\xi), \]

so it is large when the strip is wide or the fourth derivative \(f^{(4)}\) (how violently the curve bends) is large. Shrinking \(h\) helps a lot (the factor \(h^5\)).

How do we find them, without knowing \(f^{(4)}\)?

We cannot compute \(f^{(4)}\) from a table of values, so we use a trick: compute each strip's area twice and compare. On a strip \([a,b]\):

  • \(S_1\): Simpson with one parabola over the whole strip.
  • \(S_2\): Simpson on the two halves, added together (a more accurate answer).

If the curve is smooth, \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) nearly agree. If the strip is wiggly, they disagree, and the disagreement \(|S_2-S_1|\) tells us how bad the error is. Halving the strip cuts the Simpson error by about \(2^4=16\), which gives the key estimate

📐 The error estimate
\[ \text{error of } S_2 \;\approx\; \frac{|S_2-S_1|}{15}. \]
(Why 15? If the error of \(S_1\) is \(16\) times the error of \(S_2\), then \(S_2-S_1\) equals \(15\) times the error of \(S_2\).)

The algorithm

  1. Choose a tolerance tol for the whole integral (for example \(10^{-6}\)).
  2. On the current strip compute \(S_1\) and \(S_2\).
  3. If \(|S_2-S_1|/15\) is below the strip's share of the tolerance, the strip is settled: keep \(S_2\) (and often improve it to \(S_2+(S_2-S_1)/15\)).
  4. Otherwise split the strip in half, give each half half of the tolerance, and repeat from step 2 on each half.

Smooth parts settle immediately with wide strips; wiggly parts keep splitting until the pieces are small enough. That is the whole idea.

Worked example: finding the hard part of \(\int_0^1\sqrt{x}\,dx\)

The exact answer is \(2/3\). The curve \(\sqrt{x}\) is very steep at \(x=0\) and gentle near \(x=1\). Compute \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) on each half of \([0,1]\):

strip\(S_1\) (one parabola)\(S_2\) (two parabolas)\(|S_2-S_1|/15\)verdict
[0, 0.5]0.2255920.2321174.3 × 10⁻⁴wiggly: split again

5.5 Ordinary differential equations

🔊 Listen to this section

A differential equation relates an unknown function to its derivatives. A first-order initial-value problem has the form

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x,y), \qquad y(x_0) = y_0. \]
📐 What is \(f(x,y)\)?
\(f(x,y)\) is simply the slope rule: give it a point \((x,y)\) and it returns the slope \(dy/dx\) of the solution passing through that point. For \(dy/dx=y\) the rule is \(f(x,y)=y\): at the point \((0,1)\) the slope is \(1\), at \((1,3)\) it is \(3\). For \(dy/dx=\cos x\) the rule is \(f(x,y)=\cos x\), which does not depend on \(y\).

The derivative tells us the slope of the solution at every point; the initial condition pins down which specific curve we are on. Integrating \(dy/dx\) alone gives a family of curves differing by a constant \(c\); the initial value selects one.

📐 Example
From \(dy/dx = -20x + 8.5\) we get \(y = -10x^2 + 8.5x + c\). The initial condition \(y(0)=1\) fixes \(c=1\), so \(y = -10x^2 + 8.5x + 1\).

What does "march forward in steps of size \(h\)" mean?

We cannot write down the solution curve, but we know where it starts \((x_0,y_0)\) and we know its slope everywhere (that is what \(f\) gives). So we build the curve one short piece at a time, from left to right:

  1. Choose a step size \(h\): a small number, such as \(0.5\) or \(0.1\). It is the horizontal distance of one piece. The \(x\)-values we visit are equally spaced: \(x_0,\ x_1=x_0+h,\ x_2=x_0+2h,\ \dots\)
  2. Start at the known point \((x_0,y_0)\).
  3. Look up the slope there, \(f(x_0,y_0)\), and move one step to the right along that slope. That gives the next point \((x_1,y_1)\).
  4. Repeat from the new point: new slope, another step of width \(h\), next point \((x_2,y_2)\), and so on until we reach the \(x\) we want.

This "step, update the slope, step again" process is what marching forward means. A smaller \(h\) means more, shorter pieces, so the pieces follow the true curve more closely (but we need more steps). It is the same "add up many small pieces" idea as numerical integration.

Marching forward for \(dy/dx=y,\ y(0)=1\) with \(h=0.5\). The vertical lines mark the grid \(x_0,x_1,\dots\), spaced \(h\) apart. Each teal piece is one step: it starts at the previous point, follows the slope there, and ends at the next orange point. The dashed grey curve is the true solution, which the method does not know; section 5.6 gives the exact formula for each step.

5.6 Euler's method

🔊 Listen to this section

The simplest one-step method. We know the solution's value \(y_i\) at \(x_i\), and the ODE tells us its slope there, \(f(x_i,y_i)\). So walk in a straight line with that slope for one step of width \(h\):

\[ y_{i+1} = y_i + h\,f(x_i, y_i), \qquad x_{i+1} = x_i + h. \]
🔎 Reading the formula, symbol by symbol
  • \(y_i\): the current height (where we are now).
  • \(f(x_i,y_i)\): the slope of the solution at the current point. Yes: \(f\) is the slope \(dy/dx\), given by the right-hand side of the ODE.
  • \(h\): the step width (how far we move to the right).
  • \(h\,f(x_i,y_i)\) = (width) × (slope) = the rise: how much \(y\) goes up over this step.
  • \(y_{i+1}\): new height = old height + rise.

Two steps by hand: \(dy/dx=y,\ y(0)=1,\ h=0.5\)

Given: \(dy/dx=y,\ y(0)=1\), step size \(h=0.5\).
Find: an estimate of \(y(1)\) by Euler's method.

Solution.

Here \(f(x,y)=y\), so the slope at each point equals its current height. We need \(x=1\), which is two steps of size \(0.5\) from \(x_0=0\).

step\(x_i\)\(y_i\)slope \(f(x_i,y_i)=y_i\)rise \(h\cdot\text{slope}\)\(y_{i+1}=y_i+\text{rise}\)
10110.5 × 1 = 0.51.5
20.51.51.50.5 × 1.5 = 0.752.25

Written as equations, the two steps are:

\[ \begin{aligned} y_1 &= y_0 + h\,f(x_0,y_0) = 1 + 0.5\,(1) = 1.5,\\[4pt] y_2 &= y_1 + h\,f(x_1,y_1) = 1.5 + 0.5\,(1.5) = 2.25. \end{aligned} \]

Final answer (a single number, the estimated height of the solution at \(x=1\)):

\[ \boxed{\,y(1)\approx 2.25\,} \]

Comment. The exact solution is \(y=e^x\), so \(y(1)=e\approx 2.718\) and the error is about \(0.47\) (roughly 17%). Euler underestimates here because the true curve bends upward while each step follows a straight line. A smaller \(h\) would shrink the error.

\(y(1)\approx \mathbf{2.25}\). The exact solution is \(y=e^x\), so \(y(1)=e\approx 2.718\). The error is \(2.718-2.25\approx 0.47\) (about 17%). Euler underestimates here because the true curve bends upward while each step follows a straight line. A smaller \(h\) would shrink this error.

Continuing gives \(3.375\) at \(x=1.5\) and \(5.0625\) at \(x=2\), the points of the marching figure in 5.5. Each straight piece uses a slope computed at its left end. The true curve bends upward, so the straight pieces fall below it and the error builds up.

Each step uses a tangent-line approximation, so Euler accumulates error: it is first-order (halving \(h\) roughly halves the error). Smaller steps are more accurate but cost more evaluations. You can try this yourself in the demo right below this section ("Euler's method & step size").

5.7 Runge–Kutta & higher-order methods

🔊 Listen to this section

Euler uses the slope only at the start of each step. Runge–Kutta methods sample the slope several times across the step and combine them, capturing curvature Euler misses. The classic RK4 averages four slope estimates:

\[ y_{i+1} = y_i + \frac{h}{6}\bigl(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4\bigr), \]
\[ k_1 = f(x_i,y_i),\ \ k_2 = f\!\left(x_i+\tfrac{h}{2}, y_i+\tfrac{h}{2}k_1\right),\ \ k_3 = f\!\left(x_i+\tfrac{h}{2}, y_i+\tfrac{h}{2}k_2\right),\ \ k_4 = f(x_i+h, y_i+hk_3). \]

RK4 is fourth-order: halving \(h\) cuts the error by about 16×. For the same accuracy it needs far larger steps than Euler.

  • Adaptive step size: automatically shrink \(h\) where the solution changes fast and grow it where it is smooth (MATLAB's ode45 does this).
  • Coupled / higher-order ODEs: rewrite an \(n\)-th-order equation as a system of \(n\) first-order equations and march the whole vector forward together.

✏️ 5.5 Ordinary differential equations
What does the initial-value problem \(y'=f(x,y),\ y(x_0)=y_0\) ask for, and why not just integrate directly?
Reveal answer
It asks for the function \(y(x)\) whose slope everywhere equals \(f(x,y)\) and that passes through \((x_0,y_0)\).
When \(f\) depends on the unknown \(y\) itself, there is usually no closed-form antiderivative, so we march forward numerically: start at \((x_0,y_0)\) and take small slope-guided steps. the ODE gives the slope field; a solution is a curve tangent to it

✏️ 5.7 Runge–Kutta & higher-order methods
Why is classical RK4 preferred over Euler at the same step size h? What is its error order?
Reveal answer
RK4 samples the slope four times per step (start, two midpoints, end) and takes a weighted average, matching the Taylor series to 4th order.
Global error is \(O(h^{4})\) versus Euler's \(O(h)\), so it reaches the same accuracy in far fewer steps. MATLAB's ode45 is an adaptive Runge–Kutta method. more slope samples per step ⇒ much smaller error for the same h
